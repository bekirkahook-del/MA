Hedge fund interview questions and how to pitch a stock

Updated Nov 3, 2026 · M&A Prep

Hedge fund and asset management interviews center on one thing: your stock pitch. A strong pitch opens with the call (long or short, with a price target), gives two or three thesis points, explains why the market is wrong (your variant view), names a catalyst and backs the target with a valuation.

Expect follow-ups on risk: what would make you wrong, how you'd size the position, and what you'd do if it fell 20%. Know current market levels and what's driving them.

Markets and hedge fund questions

Basic

Pitch me a stock.

A strong answer covers
  • Clear long or short with a price target
  • 2–3 specific thesis points
  • Catalysts
  • Valuation support and key risks
Basic

What's going on in the markets right now?

A strong answer covers
  • Accurate current levels or trends
  • Main drivers: rates, inflation, earnings, geopolitics
  • A view on what matters next
  • Concise and confident
Basic

What's the difference between a stock and a bond?

A strong answer covers
  • Stock: ownership with a claim on profits
  • Bond: a loan with fixed payments
  • Bondholders get paid before stockholders
  • Stocks have more upside and more risk
Intermediate

How do interest rate changes affect stocks and bonds?

A strong answer covers
  • Higher rates lower bond prices
  • Higher discount rates lower stock valuations, especially growth stocks
  • Higher rates can slow the economy
  • Banks may benefit from wider margins
Intermediate

What's the difference between a hedge fund and a mutual fund?

A strong answer covers
  • Hedge funds target absolute returns; mutual funds often track benchmarks
  • Hedge funds can short, use leverage and derivatives
  • Hedge funds charge performance fees
  • Mutual funds are open to everyday investors and more regulated
Intermediate

How do you short a stock, and what are the risks?

A strong answer covers
  • Borrow shares, sell them, buy back later
  • Profit if the price falls
  • Losses are theoretically unlimited
  • Borrow costs, short squeezes and margin calls
Intermediate

Give me a stock you'd buy today and the one data point that would make you sell.

A strong answer covers
  • Clear long thesis with a price target
  • Variant view versus consensus
  • A specific, measurable sell trigger
  • Position sizing and risk awareness
Intermediate

What does the yield curve tell you right now, and why does it matter?

A strong answer covers
  • Describes current shape: steep, flat or inverted
  • Links shape to expectations for rates and growth
  • Inversion has often come before recessions
  • Impact on banks, borrowing costs and stocks
Intermediate

How would you value a bank?

A strong answer covers
  • Use equity-based metrics, not EV
  • P/E and price-to-tangible-book
  • Return on equity drives the P/B multiple
  • Dividend discount model as a cross-check
Advanced

Pitch me a short.

A strong answer covers
  • Clear thesis why the stock is overvalued
  • Catalyst that makes the market see it
  • Valuation downside
  • Risks, including squeeze risk and borrow cost
Advanced

How would you hedge a long stock position against market risk?

A strong answer covers
  • Short an index future or ETF sized by beta
  • Buy put options
  • Pair trade against a peer
  • Trade-offs in cost and basis risk
Advanced

What's your variant view on a stock you follow, and what would prove you wrong?

A strong answer covers
  • Clearly states consensus view
  • Explains how their view differs and why
  • Names specific evidence that would invalidate it
  • Shows intellectual honesty
Advanced

Oil just jumped 10%. Walk me through the second-order effects across sectors.

A strong answer covers
  • Energy producers gain; airlines, shippers and chemicals lose
  • Inflation expectations and rates may rise
  • Consumer spending power falls
  • Currency effects for oil exporters and importers
Advanced

How would you build a market-neutral pair trade in a sector you know?

A strong answer covers
  • Long the stronger company, short the weaker peer
  • Size by beta or dollar-neutral
  • Catalyst that should widen the gap
  • Risks: correlation breakdown, squeezes, borrow cost
Advanced

Walk me through how you'd size a position in a new long idea.

A strong answer covers
  • Conviction and upside versus downside
  • Volatility and correlation with the rest of the book
  • Liquidity: can you get out?
  • Start smaller and add as the thesis plays out
Advanced

A stock you're long falls 20% on earnings. What do you do?

A strong answer covers
  • Re-examine whether the thesis is broken or just delayed
  • Separate noise from new information
  • Check position size and risk limits
  • Decide to add, hold or cut, with reasons

Quick quiz (12 questions)

'Variant perception' means:
  1. Agreeing with consensus
  2. Having a view that differs from consensus ✓
  3. Changing your view often
  4. Using a different valuation method
If you agree with everyone, the opportunity is already priced in.
In a stock pitch, a catalyst is:
  1. A valuation multiple for comparing stocks
  2. An event that makes the market recognize value ✓
  3. A type of stock option
  4. A risk factor that hurts the stock
No catalyst means the stock can stay mispriced indefinitely.
Stock at $50, price target $65. Upside is:
  1. 15%
  2. 23%
  3. 30% ✓
  4. 65%
(65 − 50) ÷ 50 = 30%.
What should a stock pitch open with?
  1. The company's full history since it was founded
  2. How you feel about the company's products
  3. A clear recommendation: long or short with a price target ✓
  4. Your own portfolio's returns this year
Lead with the call, then support it.
How many thesis points is ideal in a stock pitch?
  1. One
  2. Two to three ✓
  3. Ten
  4. Zero
A few strong points beat a long list.
What is a 'variant view'?
  1. Agreeing with what most analysts think
  2. The average price target from analysts who cover the stock
  3. A stock split announced by the company
  4. Where your view differs from the market's and why ✓
If you agree with everyone, the opportunity is already priced in.
Stock at $40, target $52. Upside is:
  1. 12%
  2. 23%
  3. 30% ✓
  4. 52%
(52 − 40) ÷ 40 = 30%.
Stock at $80, target $60 on a short. Downside to target is:
  1. 20%
  2. 33%
  3. 25% ✓
  4. 60%
(80 − 60) ÷ 80 = 25%.
A fund is 120% long and 80% short. Its net exposure is:
  1. 200%
  2. 40% ✓
  3. 120%
  4. −40%
120 − 80 = 40% net long.
The Sharpe ratio measures:
  1. Total return over the year
  2. Return per unit of risk ✓
  3. Trading volume in the fund
  4. How much leverage the fund uses
Excess return divided by volatility.
A stock with beta 1.5 when the market rises 10% is expected to rise about:
  1. 5%
  2. 10%
  3. 15% ✓
  4. 25%
1.5 × 10% = 15%.
A fund is 100% long and 60% short. Its gross exposure is:
  1. 40%
  2. 100%
  3. 60%
  4. 160% ✓
100 + 60 = 160%.
